Amazon is rebuilding NBA plays in 3D with AI. During live games. This isn't a drone. It isn't a video game. It's real game footage from a camera angle that never existed. It's called Prime 360. Here's how it actually works: 1. A ring of cameras High-res cameras sit in a full perimeter around the court. Every one is calibrated and synchronized, catching the play from all angles. 2. The play becomes a 3D scene The footage goes to the AWS cloud, where the play is reconstructed as a 3D scene. Once it's a scene, the camera is free. You can fly it through the play, down on the floor, behind a shooter, right next to the ball. 3. Diffusion models paint the picture Amazon says it runs on diffusion models, the same class of AI behind image generators. Each frame gets split into dozens of overlapping tiles that go through the model one by one. 4. Custom chips make it fast enough for live TV That's billions of computations per frame. Amazon runs it on its own Trainium chips and says it cut latency by more than 40%, enough to use during live games. What people miss: This launched in October. It blew up Friday night because a Knicks vs Pacers reel had everyone saying it looks like NBA 2K. It's not on every game. Prime 360 shows up in select NBA on Prime broadcasts, in-game and in pregame, halftime and postgame breakdowns. And Amazon hasn't said how many cameras are in the ring or how long a replay takes to build. Why it matters: For years we asked when AI video would look real. This goes the other way. Real footage, rebuilt by AI, so a camera can go where no camera can. Sports broadcasts are starting to look like video games. Soon video games will look like broadcasts. Would you watch a whole game like this?
